Two cafés can both sell ₹4 lakh a month and end up in very different places. One keeps ₹1.2 lakh after rent and salaries; the other barely breaks even. Very often the difference is food cost: how much of every rupee you sell goes back out to pay for the milk, coffee, bread, paneer and packaging.

Food cost percentage is the single most useful number in a café after sales. Here is how to calculate it, what's normal, and how to bring it down without cutting portions.

> **The short answer:** food cost % = (opening stock + purchases − closing stock) ÷ food sales before GST × 100. Most cafés and casual restaurants aim for roughly 28 to 35%. Calculate it monthly, and calculate the cost of each dish once, so you know which items earn and which only keep you busy.

## The food cost formula

For a month (or any period):

**Food cost % = Cost of food used ÷ Food sales × 100**

where

**Cost of food used = Opening stock + Purchases − Closing stock**

- **Opening stock:** the value of food and beverage stock on the first day of the month
- **Purchases:** everything you bought during the month (milk, vegetables, coffee, bread, sauces, disposables used for food)
- **Closing stock:** the value of what's left on the last day
- **Food sales:** your sales **before GST**, because GST isn't your money

### Worked example

| | Amount |
|---|---|
| Opening stock on 1 November | ₹40,000 |
| Purchases during November | ₹1,10,000 |
| Closing stock on 30 November | ₹35,000 |
| **Cost of food used** | **₹1,15,000** |
| Food sales in November (before GST) | ₹3,50,000 |
| **Food cost %** | **32.9%** |

So for every ₹100 of food you sold, about ₹33 went back into ingredients.

If you skip the stock count and only use purchases, the number swings wildly: a month where you stocked up looks terrible and the next one looks great. Counting stock at month end, even roughly, makes the number honest.

## What is a good food cost percentage?

There's no single right number, because it depends on what you sell:

| Type of item | Typical food cost |
|---|---|
| Coffee, tea and most cold beverages | Often 15–25% |
| Sandwiches, pasta, snacks | Often 25–35% |
| Desserts and bakery | Often 20–35%, depends heavily on wastage |
| Meat, seafood, premium ingredients | Often 35–45% |

Many cafés land around **28 to 35%** overall. What matters more than the benchmark is your own trend: if your food cost climbs from 30% to 36% in three months, something changed, and you want to know what.

## Calculate the cost of each dish

The monthly number tells you **whether** there's a problem. Dish costing tells you **where**.

Take one portion and add up every ingredient at what you actually pay:

**Cold coffee (₹180)**

| Ingredient | Cost |
|---|---|
| Milk, 200 ml | ₹12 |
| Coffee | ₹8 |
| Sugar and ice | ₹3 |
| Cup, lid and straw | ₹7 |
| **Total** | **₹30** |

Food cost: ₹30 ÷ ₹180 = **16.7%**. A strong earner.

**Paneer sandwich (₹180)**

| Ingredient | Cost |
|---|---|
| Bread | ₹8 |
| Paneer, 60 g | ₹24 |
| Vegetables and sauces | ₹10 |
| Butter | ₹6 |
| **Total** | **₹48** |

Food cost: ₹48 ÷ ₹180 = **26.7%**. Healthy.

Do this for your top 15 to 20 dishes. Then compare with what sells most. The perfect menu has its best-selling dishes among its lowest food cost dishes.

## Pricing a dish from its cost

To price a new dish at a target food cost:

**Menu price = Dish cost ÷ Target food cost**

A dish that costs ₹60 to make, at a 30% target: ₹60 ÷ 0.30 = **₹200**. Then check it against what nearby cafés charge and what your guests will pay, and adjust the recipe or the price.

Remember that in India, prices on the menu and the bill must be clear about GST. Many cafés set menu prices before GST and add 5% on the bill. Either way, calculate food cost on the price **without** GST.

## Seven ways to lower food cost (without smaller portions)

1. **Count stock weekly for your top 10 ingredients.** Milk, paneer, chicken and coffee are where money leaks first.
2. **Write recipes and portion sizes down.** "A handful" is different for every cook. Scoops and scales keep portions the same.
3. **Track wastage.** Keep a simple sheet in the kitchen: what was thrown away and why. Patterns appear in two weeks.
4. **Cut wrong orders.** A dish made twice costs double. Orders that go straight from the guest's phone to a [kitchen screen](/kot-software/) remove most of these.
5. **Buy smarter, not just cheaper.** Compare suppliers every few months, and check that you received what you paid for.
6. **Push the dishes that earn.** Put low food cost, high-margin items where eyes land first on your menu, and train staff to suggest them.
7. **Watch discounts.** A 10% discount on a dish with 30% food cost takes away a third of your margin on that dish. Record every discount with a reason, so you can see who gives them and why.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I calculate food cost percentage?

Add your opening stock and purchases for the period, subtract your closing stock, and divide the result by your food sales before GST. Multiply by 100 to get a percentage.

### What is a good food cost percentage for a café in India?

Many cafés aim for roughly 28 to 35% overall. Beverage-heavy cafés can run lower, and menus with a lot of meat or premium ingredients run higher. Watch your own trend month to month.

### Should I include GST when calculating food cost?

No. Use sales before GST, because the GST you collect is passed on to the government. Including it makes your food cost look lower than it really is.

### How often should I calculate food cost?

Monthly for the overall percentage, and whenever an ingredient price changes for your key dishes. A weekly check on your top 10 ingredients catches problems early.
